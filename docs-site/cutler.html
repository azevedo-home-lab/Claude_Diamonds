Prioritization — Cutler

Force-ranked 1-N bets instead of scored buckets: an argument made out loud and recorded, checked by ten named traps. This page teaches the method for solo use and for facilitating it in a workshop.

3 primary sources 8 sections Sources: TBM 399 · TBM 230
01

The whole method on one page

You have N candidate pieces of work. The method turns them into one decided, defensible order.

The blue box is where the decision is made; the amber box is where it is challenged; the green box is the first moment the rank is ratifiable.

Two sentences to remember. Rank is the decision; buckets are presentation. And: you haven't prioritized anything until you can name what it displaces.
02

Why: the stance

Cutler is framework-skeptical, not framework-free. The target is false rigor.

Scoring systems — RICE, weighted matrices, estimation bingo — tend to produce a number so nobody has to have the hard conversation. The weights get gamed, the scores get horse-traded, and nobody ever checks whether the work delivered its outcome. What replaces the score is an argument, made out loud and recorded: "this ranks above that because of value, urgency, and what waiting costs — and here is what we are explicitly not doing as a result."

Three beliefs underneath everything. First, rank is the decision; buckets are presentation. A "Now" bucket with three items dodges the question of which drops when capacity runs short — deferring the hard conversation to the worst possible moment, mid-execution. A 1-N rank cannot dodge: when time runs out, the bottom drops, and that was decided calmly, in advance. Second, prioritizing means deprioritizing. Every yes is a no to something; if you can't name the no, you haven't decided anything. Third, good-enough beats perfect. The aim is marginally better decisions made quickly and revisited — not a perfect ordering defended forever.

03

Building blocks

One unit of work, four argument lenses, and one tool demoted to scaffolding.

A bet is a unit of prioritized work framed as a wager, not a promise — calling it a bet admits it can lose. Every bet carries three attachments that close its loop:

AttachmentQuestion it answers
OwnerWho is accountable for closing the loop?
Expected outcomeWhat changes if this wins? (traced to strategy — the Decision Stack's upward why)
CheckpointWhen do we look — a date, a learning milestone, or a decision point, set in advance?

Every ranking argument uses the four lenses, and only these — never personal preference:

LensThe question
ValueWhich moves the outcome more?
UrgencyWhich one can't wait — and why?
Cost of delay + shapeWhat does a month of waiting cost? Linear (steady bleed) or cliff (fine until a date, then value collapses)? Cliffs justify urgency; bleeds justify scheduling, not panic.
Opportunity costWhat does each bet displace? (the "Displaces" column)

Impact-vs-effort — the familiar 2×2 — survives in this method only as the entry sort: a cheap way to get a draft order. Never the verdict.

04

The machine

There is no formula. Ranking is manual sorting with arguments as the comparator — three phases.

Phase 1 — Entry sort (~10 min, decides nothing)

Gut-place every candidate on impact-vs-effort and read off a first 1-N line. Why bother? Arguing every possible pair is O(N²) — 8 bets means 28 comparisons. A gut sort gets the order roughly right so the real arguing happens only at borderline neighbors. It's scaffolding; nothing from this phase is recorded. Before sorting, check the list itself is complete — the invisible candidates must be on it: systemic friction, deferred big options, maintenance. A perfect sort of an incomplete list is still wrong.

Phase 2 — Pairwise argument (the real decision)

Walk the line top-down. For each adjacent pair ask one question: "If I could only do one of these two next month — which, and why?" The "why" must be argued in the four lenses. If the lower bet wins, swap. Re-walk until no swap survives. The final rank IS this accumulated pile of small pairwise arguments — nothing more mystical than that.

The tie rule. If a pair genuinely can't be ordered, you're not stuck — you're missing information. Name it ("is this pain 1h/week or 1h/day?"). Getting that answer is itself work, usually cheap.

Worked example. Bet A: an honesty gate (a verifier that cannot fake a green result). Bet B: a shared STOP vocabulary (every halt names a human action). One month, one bet — which? Value: A guards the outcome itself; B saves debug time — A leads. Cost of delay: A is a near-cliff — every merge until then carries fake-green risk, and one fake green destroys trust in the whole system; B bleeds linearly, one wasted debug session per stall. Displacement: both cheap to name. Verdict: A above B — and the argument, not a score, goes in the log.

Phase 3 — Trap check (the adversary, ~15 min)

Pairwise arguing has a blind spot: it can perfectly order a wrong list. The trap check catches what arguing can't. Read all ten trap names against the rank as a whole — not per bet, so the cost is fixed regardless of N. Some traps point at specific bets when they fire (3: who claims to displace nothing? 8: which big option sank to the bottom out of fear?). Others check what's missing from the list entirely (1: where's the protected slice? 7: which friction never became a bet? 10: which bets are invented work?). Each hit adds a bet, forces a swap, or attaches a fix. State the no-hits too — a check that only reports hits invites skipping. Only after the trap check is the rank ratifiable.

Closing out

Attach owner, outcome, and checkpoint to each surviving bet. Record the arguments in the log — never scores. Fold into Now/Next/Later for communication only. Revisit only on new evidence, as a new log entry; relitigating a decided rank without new evidence is churn.

05

The ten traps

From TBM 399. Each named after a song; each has a remedy. Scan by name, every session.

#Trap (song)Failure patternRemedy
1Burning Down the HouseAll capacity reactive; firefighting eats everythingProtect a fixed slice (10–20%) for preventative work
2Too Much Time on My HandsOver-polishing medium-value workDefine the smallest version that teaches something real in 2–4 weeks
3Everybody Wants to Rule the World"Top priority" that displaces nothingName one thing explicitly allowed to drop, out loud
4Just Enough Is Never EnoughMVP ships, follow-on never funded, value never capturedPre-authorize one follow-on investment at the start
5Running on EmptyDeath march with no off-track signalSet a date, learning milestone, or decision checkpoint in advance
6DreamerInnovation with no constraints, drifts foreverAdd one enabling constraint (deadline to ship real, forced integration)
7Slow RideInvisible systemic friction never makes the listQuantify the drag; reframe as cost of delay, not "cleanup"
8Someday Never ComesBig options deferred for lack of confidenceRun one cheap experiment aimed only at reducing uncertainty
9The Logical SongFrameworks favor high-confidence, untestable workAsk "how cheaply can we raise confidence?"; favor fast learning
10Takin' Care of BusinessInvented work fills capacityKeep a small-batch pull queue of accepted, needle-moving work

Memory aid — the traps come in rough pairs: 1 & 10 are about what fills capacity (reaction / invention); 2 & 4 about investment sizing (too much polish / too little follow-through); 5 & 6 about missing boundaries (no exit signal / no constraints); 7 & 8 about what never gets picked (friction / scary big options). 3 is the honesty trap (no displacement) and 9 the framework trap (the tool itself biases the choice).

Solo-portfolio regulars: 1, 3, 7, 8. When time is short, scan those four at minimum — every session. For one builder they fire far more often than the rest.
06

After the rank: accountability

Source: TBM 230. Ranking is the small piece.

Some uncomfortable share of prioritized work will fail to produce its intended outcome — and most organizations never find out, because they're "too busy with the next planning cycle." The loop never closes. Cutler's move: treat funding like startup-style progressive investment, not an internal-contractor game (deliver features, ask for more budget). Every bet has an owner accountable for closing the loop, not just shipping. Checkpoints are set in advance, and looking at them is scheduled work, not an afterthought. Prove value small before funding big — bets earn continued investment at checkpoints by showing evidence. And if you don't set up this governance yourself, someone else eventually imposes a worse version — so it might as well be you.

In Claude Diamonds

How the front diamond's Prioritize + sequence zone applies the method. The binding rules live in cutler-prioritization.claude.md.

Method conceptConcrete artifact
The force-ranked betsThe plan section of the vision/OST doc — a 1-N table, one row per bet
Owner / outcome / checkpointColumns in that table: Outcome, Cost of delay, Displaces, Checkpoint
The recorded argumentA decision-log entry per rank decision — the argument, never a score
Now/Next/LaterOne presentation line under the table — communication only
Re-rank on new evidenceA new decision-log entry citing the evidence; old entries never rewritten
The trap checkRun by name each session on the current rank, before ratification

Four local additions, because one builder plus an AI fails differently than a team: no ties, and an unorderable pair must name its missing conversation; trap scan minimum 1/3/7/8 every session, even when time is short; append-only history, so the trail of why survives (scores destroy this; arguments preserve it); and the AI must challenge before agreeing — the adversarial protocol substitutes for the colleagues who would push back in a team.

08

Self-test

If you can answer these from memory, you can run the method — solo or in a room.

  1. Why is a tie in the rank forbidden, and what does a tie actually signal?
  2. What are the two shapes of cost of delay, and why does shape matter more than size?
  3. "Rank is the decision; buckets are presentation" — what conversation does a bucket let you dodge, and when does that dodge hurt?
  4. Which trap does an empty "Displaces" column indicate, and what's the remedy?
  5. What three things must be attached to every bet, and which trap does each guard against?
  6. What's wrong with using impact-vs-effort as the final verdict?
  7. Does the trap check judge each bet individually? What does it actually scan?
  8. When is re-ranking legitimate, and when is it churn?
