Claude Diamonds — method foundations

Opportunity Solution Tree — Torres

One tree per product: a measurable outcome, customer opportunities sourced from evidence, three compared solutions per target, and small assumption tests that let evidence — not argument — kill the losers.

2 primary sources 8 sections Sources: Product Talk — OST · Assumption Testing
01

The whole method on one page

Four levels, strictly ordered, revised every 3–4 interviews. Never finished — used.

The loop: target opportunity → ~3 candidate solutions → decompose into assumptions → test the riskiest → discard / refine / retarget. Repeat, revising every 3–4 interviews.

Two sentences to remember. Opportunities come from customers, not from the team's imagination. And: compare-and-contrast beats evaluate-in-isolation — always three solutions, never one.
02

Why: the stance

The tree is the antidote to two failure modes — and the frame for continuous discovery.

The first failure mode is the solution-first reflex: someone arrives with a feature idea, the team builds it, and only afterwards discovers which problem — if any — it solved. The tree forces every solution to hang under an evidenced customer opportunity, which hangs under a measurable outcome. If a solution can't name its opportunity, it has no branch to hang on.

The second is whether-or-not decisions: evaluating one idea in isolation ("should we build this?"), which produces measurably worse decisions than comparing alternatives ("which of these three best addresses the target?"). The tree structurally prevents single-option thinking: three solutions per target, compared, with tests that let evidence kill the losers.

Underneath both: continuous discovery. Not a research phase before building, but story-based interviews week over week, the tree revised as evidence arrives, decisions made on the freshest picture available.

03

Building blocks

Four node types, one test that separates the middle two, and five flavors of assumption.

The outcome (root) is a product outcome: a measurable customer behavior or sentiment the team can move. One per team/product at a time. Two look-alikes to reject: business outcomes (revenue, churn) — too broad for the team to move directly; traction metrics (clicks on a feature) — too narrow to discover against.

An opportunity is a customer need, pain point, or desire that, if addressed, drives the outcome — sourced from story-based customer input, structured by an experience map first (the customer's moments, in order), then arranged in parent/child branches.

The opportunity-vs-solution test: is there more than one way to address it? If only one, it's a solution wearing an opportunity's clothes. "Users need a dashboard" → solution. "Users can't tell whether yesterday's run succeeded" → opportunity.

A solution is anything offered to address a known opportunity — feature, service, workflow, document. Effort is allowed into the conversation here, and only here. An assumption is a statement that must be true for a solution to work, in five flavors: desirability (do customers want it?), viability (does it benefit the business?), feasibility (can we build it?), usability (can customers use it?), ethical (could it harm?). The categories exist to surface diverse risks, not to be debated. An assumption test is a small, fast experiment on ONE assumption — never the whole idea — with success criteria defined before running, so results can't be rationalized afterwards.

04

The machine

Prerequisites, then a five-step loop that never finishes — it converges per target and starts again.

Prerequisites

A theory of who the customer is and what value you offer; a clearly defined outcome; at least three story-based interviews. A tree built on zero interviews is a diagram of the team's imagination.

Step 1 — Map the experience, then the opportunities

From interview stories, draw the experience map. Map top-level opportunities to moments; group individual needs under them as children. Keep it a crummy first draft — pick a target early and revise as evidence arrives. If the tree is still messy after 6–8 interviews, restructure then.

What a map looks like, concretely — moments in sequence, an emotional layer, and pains that cluster at specific moments. This one maps "delegating one feature to an AI workflow":

Pains cluster at specific moments; each cluster became an opportunity on the tree (O-numbers from the worked example in §07). The moments — not the features — are the map's spine.

The discipline for drawing one: scope the experience by the outcome — the journey the outcome lives in, not the customer's whole life. Draw individually first — each person sketches before seeing anyone else's map, so the loudest voice doesn't anchor the room. Moments, not features — every node is something the customer does or experiences; a node containing your product's name is your solution wearing the customer's clothes. Add the emotional layer — where friction clusters is where opportunities live. Validate against stories — the map is a hypothesis until interviews confirm it, revised on the same 3–4-interview cadence as the tree.

Step 2 — Select ONE target opportunity

A genuine prioritization moment — comparing sibling opportunities, argued not scored, across four factor groups: opportunity sizing (how many customers, how often), market factors, company factors (fit with vision and strategy), and customer factors (importance; satisfaction with existing alternatives). The pairwise-argument mechanics from the prioritization lesson transfer directly — same sorting, different lenses. Note what is not evaluated here: assumptions. An assumption exists only relative to a solution, and no solutions are in play yet.

The effort rule — the method's hardest constraint. Effort does NOT enter opportunity selection. "That one looks expensive" is a statement about solutions you haven't compared yet. Effort enters one level down, never here.

Step 3 — Generate ~3 solutions, together

For the target opportunity only. All three must genuinely address it — re-run the opportunity-vs-solution test on each. If someone arrived solution-first, reverse it: which opportunity is this for, and what else would address it? At least two alternatives beside it before any comparing happens.

Step 4 — Decompose into assumptions, map them, test the riskiest

4a — Decompose: this is where the five flavors live. For each of the three candidates, ask "what must be true for this to work?" — walking the five categories from §03 as a generative checklist: desirable (do customers want it?), viable (does it benefit the business?), feasible (can we build it?), usable (can customers use it?), ethical (could it harm?). The categories are not scores and not gates — they exist to surface risks from every direction, not just the comfortable one.

4b — Map and pick. Place every surfaced assumption on Torres's 2×2 (adopted from David Bland): importance to the idea's success × strength of existing evidence. High importance + weak evidence is what you test first — the same entry-sort trick as impact-vs-effort in the ranking method: a fast quadrant placement that produces a test order, not a verdict. Test small: one assumption, one test, criteria written down first, first test doable within an hour. Never decide from a single test — look for patterns across tests and across the competing solutions.

The contrast with Step 2 is the point: assumptions exist only relative to a solution, so none of this applies to opportunity selection — and the sharpest proof is feasibility, a legitimate flavor here but exactly the banned question there (the effort rule).

Step 5 — Close the loop

Each test kills, refines, or promotes candidates. When a solution survives its riskiest assumptions, it leaves the tree and becomes a bet — and the Cutler machine takes over. When all candidates die, return to Step 3 — or retarget to a different opportunity.

06

The Cutler handoff

The two methods split the front diamond's deciding work — same sorting mechanics, never the same comparator.

QuestionMethodComparator
What's worth betting on?OSTEvidence — interviews, assumption tests
What first?Cutler rankArgument — value, urgency, cost of delay, displacement

Quadrant entry sorts and pairwise comparison transfer between the methods; the comparator never does. Inside the tree, evidence decides and arguing is a smell; inside the rank, argument decides because the evidence work is already done. The three OST moments where prioritization mechanics legitimately appear: choosing the target opportunity (pairwise, four factors), ordering assumption tests (importance × evidence quadrant), and — deliberately never — choosing between solutions, where tests kill and arguments don't.

07

In Claude Diamonds

How the front diamond's Framing zone applies the tree. The binding rules live in opportunity-solution-tree.claude.md.

Framing means producing or extending the OST — never writing a solution pitch. The framing artifact names: outcome, opportunity + evidence, siblings considered, then candidate solutions. Every opportunity carries an evidence tag: interview-sourced, proxy-sourced (tickets, analytics, the Owner's domain experience — legal but named), or assumed — and an assumed node cannot be a target; it becomes an open-questions entry instead. When the Owner arrives with a solution, it gets reversed: which opportunity, for whom, what else would address it — at least two alternatives beside it before any prioritization. Assumption tests are desk-scale evidence (a throwaway prototype, a data pull, an interview question) — never a spec; the promotion gate is unchanged. Target-opportunity choices go in the decision log with the four selection factors stated — the argument, never a score.

Worked example. A current product's tree runs: Outcome ("merge on a green verdict without re-reading the diff") → seven opportunities, each tagged interview (Owner-named pain) or proxy (recorded gaps, vendor-confirmed mechanisms) → candidate solutions compared per target — one opportunity's table shows a full compare-and-contrast of three approaches, with the winner chosen on argued evidence — and the survivors became the eight force-ranked bets of the plan.
08

Self-test

If you can answer these from memory, you can run the tree — solo or in a room.

  1. What makes an outcome a product outcome, and why are business outcomes and traction metrics both rejected as roots?
  2. State the opportunity-vs-solution test. Apply it to "customers need dark mode."
  3. Why is effort banned from target-opportunity selection, and where does it enter instead?
  4. Why three solutions instead of one? What does comparison buy that isolation can't?
  5. Name the five assumption categories, and the two axes of the assumption map. In which step does each set operate, and why can't they merge?
  6. What must be defined before an assumption test runs, and what mistake does it prevent?
  7. When does a solution leave the tree, and which method takes over at that moment?
  8. Your tree has a node "build a mobile app." Which anti-patterns fire, and what do you do?
